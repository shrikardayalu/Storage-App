Somewhere in your iPhone's camera settings is a choice between two formats with unhelpful names. It quietly decides how big every new photo and video is, so it's worth thirty seconds.

## What the two options mean

Open **Settings → Camera → Formats**. Under Camera Capture you'll see:

| | High Efficiency | Most Compatible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Photos saved as | HEIF (files end in .heic) | JPEG |
| Videos saved as | HEVC, also called H.265 | H.264 |
| Storage used | Less | More |
| Opens on | Apple devices and most current phones, computers, and apps | Practically everything, including old software |

HEIF and HEVC are newer ways of compressing the same picture. [Apple's support page on the formats](https://support.apple.com/en-us/116944) says they offer better compression than JPEG and H.264, so they use less storage on your device and in iCloud Photos "while preserving the same visual quality."

Support for both arrived with iOS 11, and High Efficiency is what an iPhone uses unless you've changed it.

## Which one should you pick?

**Pick High Efficiency if** you're short on storage, you pay for iCloud space, or you mostly share with other iPhone users. That's most people.

**Pick Most Compatible if** you regularly move files by cable to a device or program that can't read .heic or HEVC, and converting each time would drive you up the wall: an older Windows PC, a digital photo frame, a school or work upload form that only takes JPEG.

If you're only occasionally sending a photo to someone on another kind of phone, you don't need to switch. Your iPhone handles that for you, as the next section explains.

## Sharing without switching formats

You can keep the smaller files and still send copies that open anywhere.

- **AirDrop, Messages, and email:** if the other device doesn't support HEIF or HEVC, Apple says the photo or video might be shared as JPEG or H.264 automatically.
- **Importing to a Mac or PC by cable:** files might be converted to JPEG or H.264 on the way. If you'd rather get the untouched originals, go to **Settings → Apps → Photos**, scroll down, and tap **Keep Originals**.
- **iCloud Photos:** everything keeps its original format, resolution, and frame rate.
- **On a Mac:** open the photo in Photos or Preview, choose **File → Export**, and pick JPEG.

None of these change the original in your library. They make a compatible copy for whoever is receiving it.

## What changes when you switch

- **Only new shots are affected.** Nothing already in your library is converted, in either direction. Switching to High Efficiency today won't shrink last year's videos.
- **Some video modes need High Efficiency.** On many iPhones, the most demanding options under **Settings → Camera → Record Video** only appear with High Efficiency selected. If a mode such as 4K at 60 fps has vanished, this is usually why.
- **Storage estimates change.** The per-minute sizes listed at the bottom of the **Record Video** screen reflect the format you've chosen. Flip the setting and look again to see the difference on your own model.

## See the difference on your own iPhone

Sizes vary with the scene, so the honest test is your own camera:

1. Take a photo with **High Efficiency** selected.
2. Switch to **Most Compatible** and take the same photo again.
3. Open each one in **Photos** and swipe up (or tap the **ⓘ** button). The info panel shows the format and the file size.
4. Switch back to whichever setting you want to keep.

## The format is only part of your storage

Format decides how efficiently each file is stored. Resolution and frame rate decide how much there is to store, and for video they matter more. A minute of 4K at 60 fps is several times the size of a minute of 1080p at 30 fps in either format.
